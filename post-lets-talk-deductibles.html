Let’s Talk Deductibles

Health Insurance Deductibles: What They Mean and How They Work

Understanding your health insurance deductible can help you prepare for medical expenses and avoid surprises. But your deductible is only one piece of the picture. Knowing how it connects to copays, coinsurance, and your out-of-pocket maximum helps you understand what you may actually pay when you need care.

What Is a Health Insurance Deductible?

A deductible is the amount you pay for covered services that are subject to the deductible before your insurance begins sharing those costs.

For example, if your plan has a $2,000 deductible, you generally pay the first $2,000 of eligible expenses for services that require you to meet it. Some benefits may be covered before you reach that amount, depending on your plan.

You meet your deductible through eligible medical expenses as you receive care. It is separate from your premium, which is the amount you pay each month to maintain coverage.

How Does a Deductible Work?

Let’s say you have a $2,000 deductible and have not paid anything toward it yet. You receive a covered, in-network service with an insurance-negotiated price of $800. If the service is subject to your deductible, you pay $800, leaving $1,200 of your deductible remaining.

Your responsibility is generally based on the plan’s negotiated or allowed amount for covered, in-network care.

Does Insurance Cover Anything Before You Meet Your Deductible?

Yes, it can. Some plans cover certain office visits or prescriptions with a copay before you meet your deductible. Marketplace plans also cover certain preventive services without requiring you to meet the deductible when coverage requirements are satisfied.

Check your benefits carefully: some plans have separate prescription deductibles, and family coverage may include both individual and family deductibles.

What Happens After You Meet Your Deductible?

After meeting your deductible, you may still owe a portion of your medical costs through:

  • Copays: A fixed amount for a covered service, such as $30.
  • Coinsurance: A percentage of the covered cost, such as 20%.

For example, if your coinsurance is 20% and a covered service has an allowed cost of $1,000, you pay $200 and your insurance pays $800 after the applicable deductible has been met.

How Does the Deductible Count Toward Your Out-of-Pocket Maximum?

Your out-of-pocket maximum limits how much you pay for covered, in-network care during a plan year. For Marketplace plans, eligible deductible payments, copays, and coinsurance count toward this limit.

Your deductible is included in your out-of-pocket maximum. If your deductible is $2,000 and your out-of-pocket maximum is $6,000, that does not mean you must pay $8,000. The eligible $2,000 you paid toward your deductible is already part of the $6,000 limit.

An Example: From Deductible to Out-of-Pocket Maximum

Imagine a plan with these benefits:

Plan featureAmount
Deductible$2,000
Your coinsurance after the deductible20%
Out-of-pocket maximum$6,000

Assume you start with no accumulated expenses and receive $30,000 in covered, in-network care. All services in this example are subject to the same deductible and coinsurance.

  1. You pay the first $2,000, meeting your deductible.
  2. You then pay 20% of additional covered costs while your plan pays 80%.
  3. Once you have paid another $4,000 in coinsurance, your total eligible spending reaches $6,000.
  4. Your plan pays 100% of covered, in-network costs for the rest of the plan year.

In this hypothetical example, your share of the $30,000 is $6,000, and the plan pays $24,000. Your monthly premiums remain separate. This illustrates how deductible and coinsurance payments accumulate toward the out-of-pocket limit.

What Does Not Count Toward the Maximum?

The in-network out-of-pocket maximum generally excludes:

  • Monthly premiums.
  • Services your plan does not cover.
  • Out-of-network expenses.
  • Charges above the plan’s allowed amount, when you can legally be billed for them.

That means your out-of-pocket maximum is a limit on eligible cost sharing—not a cap on every health-related expense.

What Should You Look for When Comparing Plans?

Look at the monthly premium, deductible, copays, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket maximum together. A lower deductible alone does not tell you which plan will cost less over the year. Your expected doctor visits, prescriptions, and other care also matter.

Before enrolling, review the plan’s Summary of Benefits and Coverage to understand which services require the deductible and which expenses count toward its limits. The explanations above describe typical comprehensive coverage; other types of health coverage may work differently.
